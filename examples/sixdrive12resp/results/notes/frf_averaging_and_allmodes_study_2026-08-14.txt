Continuation of frf_change_study_2026-08-14.txt (the two-mode switch study).
Same system: 6-drive/12-response frame, M=8 control channels (nodes 7-14),

===============================================================================
PART A: SYSTEM ID AVERAGING SWEEP (baseline/nominal system, no FRF switch)
===============================================================================
Motivation: with TF-update on, section 3 of the two-mode write-up found a
persistent, unbounded closed-loop identification bias (H1 drifts away from
truth over time because the excitation is shaped by the control law, not
broadband). Question: does more averaging help fight this, as naive intuition
suggests, or does it make it worse (lag against a moving target)?

Method: nominal (unshifted) system, TF-update ON throughout, live "Response
Error (dB)" panel read directly off the GUI after each config had stabilized
(7-26+ minutes per run depending on config). Each run relaunched fresh
(profile reloaded, hardware reinitialized) to avoid carrying over state.

--- A.1 Linear averaging sweep ---
  Averages(N) | ch2-6 range (dB) | midpoint
       10     |    3.0 - 3.7     |   3.35
       20     |    3.7 - 4.6     |   4.15
       40     |    4.7 - 5.4     |   5.05
       80     |    5.4 - 6.6     |   6.00

Monotonic across all four points: MORE averaging is CONSISTENTLY WORSE. Not
noise -- confirmed by a clean, repeatable trend across 4 independent runs.

Mechanism, verified against the actual code (components/spectral_processing.py
:214-326): "Linear" averaging is a true sliding boxcar window of the most
recent N blocks, not a cumulative running mean:
    S(f,t) = (1/N) * sum_{k=t-N+1}^{t} X_k(f) X_k(f)^H
A boxcar average of the last N samples has an effective "center of mass" lag
of about N/2 blocks behind current time. Since H is drifting roughly
monotonically (unbounded, per the 2026-07-29 finding) rather than settling to
a fixed noisy value, that lag translates into bias(t) ~ (N/2)*dH/dt -- a
PERMANENT lag-proportional-to-N bias, not a transient that patience fixes.
Confirmed with the user that even after long run times, the higher-N configs
did not catch up to the lower-N ones -- consistent with an unbounded drift
having no steady state to converge to.

Interesting secondary observation: the increment between successive sweep
points is roughly CONSTANT (~0.8-0.95 dB) even though N is DOUBLING each
step (10->20->40->80), suggestive of error ~ log(N) rather than strictly
linear in N. Noted as a tentative pattern (4 single-run realizations, not
repeated trials) rather than a confirmed law.

--- A.2 A related knob not swept live: effective delta-f ---
delta_f = sample_rate / samples_per_frame (currently 1 Hz, 1-second frames).
The real-time "memory" of the averaging window is:
    T_window (seconds) = N_averages * T_frame = N_averages / delta_f
N and delta_f are NOT independent for the lag-vs-drift tradeoff -- they
multiply together. Finer delta_f (longer frames) makes a given N represent
MORE real time (worse lag, same effect as raising N); coarser delta_f (shorter
frames) buys real-time responsiveness at fixed N, at the cost of frequency
resolution (risk of not resolving closely-spaced modes -- relevant given this
system has mode pairs only ~2-4 Hz apart, see Part B) and more leakage.
Coarser delta_f also roughly halves total in-band bin count per Hz coarsened,
so full SDP convergence (n_deferred=0) happens in proportionally fewer calls.
Not swept live this session; flagged as a natural 4th axis if revisited.

--- A.3 Exponential averaging ---
Formula (verified in code, spectral_processing.py "else" branch):
    S_new = alpha*X_this_frame + (1-alpha)*S_old
Effective equivalent linear-average count, from matching steady-state
variance: N_eff = (2-alpha)/alpha ~= 2/alpha - 1.

  Config              | N_eff | ch2-6 range (dB) | midpoint | Drive RMS
  Exponential a=0.1    | ~19   |   3.3 - 4.3      |   3.75   |   ~3.8
  Exponential a=0.2    |  ~9   |   2.3 - 2.7      |   2.5    |   ~70 (!)

At a=0.1 (N_eff~19, close to Linear-20's N_eff=20): Exponential clearly beats
Linear at matched effective window (3.75 vs 4.15 dB midpoint). Consistent
with theory -- geometrically-decaying weights favor recent data more than a
boxcar's flat equal-weighting, so less effective lag per unit of
noise-smoothing for the same nominal "memory".

At a=0.2 (N_eff~9, close to Linear-10's N_eff=10): error improved further
to the best result in the whole sweep (2.3-2.7 dB) -- but drive RMS exploded
to ~70 (vs ~4 for every other config tested). This is NOT a favorable
trade -- ~1 dB further improvement over a=0.1 for a ~17x drive cost. Revised
interpretation: at a=0.1 the "sees the true (less-lagged) structure more
accurately" explanation fit the modest drive cost well; at a=0.2 the estimate
is likely too fast/noisy for the SDP to distinguish real fast-drifting
structure from raw per-block estimation noise, and it starts chasing noise
with expensive drive corrections -- same qualitative mechanism as buzz's
original pinv blowup near an ill-conditioned direction, just triggered by a
jittery estimate here instead of a static ill-conditioned one. There is
apparently a regime-change threshold somewhere between a=0.1 and a=0.2.

Chose a=0.15 (N_eff~12) as the working point for Part B's live FRF-switch
run: fast enough to react promptly to a real switch, with more margin below
the apparent noise-chasing threshold than a=0.2 gives.

PART B: ALL-MODES SHIFTED SYSTEM
===============================================================================
Second FRF-change scenario, larger/more diffuse than the two-mode study:
ALL 33 flexible modes shifted simultaneously instead of just 2.

--- B.1 Construction ---
  - First 4 modes: deterministic -- frequency +4%, damping -20% of baseline
    (e.g. mode 1: 120.00->124.80 Hz, 4.00%->3.20%)
  - Remaining 29 modes: random -- frequency perturbed N(0, 20 Hz) additive,
    damping perturbed N(0, 25%) multiplicative relative to baseline (seed=42)
  Built with the same exact rank-1 modal update as the two-mode file
  (examples/sixdrive12resp/code/build_shifted_frf_system_allmodes.py).

Real bug found and fixed during construction: the first verification pass
re-solved the full eigenproblem and compared modes by SORTED frequency order.
With 33 modes (several pairs only a few Hz apart) and +/-20 Hz random shifts,
18 of 33 modes swapped sorted-order position with a neighbor ("mode
veering") -- a real, harmless physical outcome, but it silently corrupted
the index-by-sorted-index comparison (reported a spurious 39 Hz "error").
Worse, it was a REAL bug, not just a reporting one: the damping-shift step
was built using the re-sorted eigenvectors, so for any crossed pair it would
have applied one mode's damping target to a different (crossed) mode's
eigenvector. Fixed by using each mode's own ORIGINAL eigenvector throughout
(proven to remain an exact eigenvector of the shifted system regardless of
sort order, via the same M-orthogonality argument as the two-mode file) and
its exact target frequency, rather than the re-solved/re-sorted
eigendecomposition. After the fix: max frequency error 1.1e-13 Hz, max
damping error 3.5e-17 against each mode's own eigenvector -- exact.

Wired into the same live-switch mechanism as the two-mode file via new
Makefile variable FRF_SWITCH_FILE_ALLMODES and recipes
build-shifted-system-allmodes / launch-rattlesnake-frf-study-allmodes
(switch-frf and log-frf-switch are shared/scenario-agnostic).

--- B.2 Live switch run (Exponential a=0.15, TF-update ON) ---
Pre-switch baseline (nominal system, stabilized):
    ch2-6 = 3.05 - 3.48 dB, drive RMS ~3.85
  (Sits between Linear-10 and Exponential-0.1 from Part A, as expected for
  N_eff~12 -- confirms 0.15 behaves sanely, nowhere near a=0.2's blowup.)

Fired the switch (all 33 modes). Self-predicted diagnostic:
    right after switch (call #490): [9.11  6.40 10.92  5.71 10.53  5.53 18.77 17.13]
    ~200 calls later   (call #693): [13.66  7.41  6.74  6.39 19.00 11.03 25.16 24.58]
"Nothing changed" was the user's first impression -- correct for the first
~10 calls, but wrong over a longer window: it took time to show up because
(1) Exponential smoothing blends in a sudden real change gradually rather
than jumping instantly (ramps in over roughly N_eff blocks minimum), and
(2) the SDP only re-solves 10 drift-protected bins/call, so cycling the
full 901-bin band through the new reality takes many calls regardless of
how fast the underlying estimate itself reacts.

Live "Response Error (dB)" panel: spiked noticeably right after the switch
(control law briefly driving against a stale pre-switch H against the now-
different true system), then RECOVERED/decreased over several minutes to a
settled plateau -- confirmed by the user watching it stabilize rather than
still trending down:
    Settled post-switch: ch2-6 = 3.63 - 4.86 dB, drive RMS ~3.66 (unchanged)

--- B.4 Fresh-start comparison (resolves B.3) ---
Launched Rattlesnake with Hardware File pointed DIRECTLY at the all-modes-
shifted system from the start (system ID done fresh against the already-
shifted system, no live switch transient at all), same Exponential a=0.15 /
TF-update ON settings, specifically to isolate whether the B.3 settled cost
was inherent to the shifted system's difficulty or a residual artifact of
the switch transition itself.

Result, confirmed settled (two checks 3+ minutes apart, essentially
identical -- drive RMS 8.32 both times, ch2-6 and ch7-8 within ~0.1 dB):
    ch2-6 = 1.85 - 2.56 dB, ch7-8 = 5.1 - 7.9 dB, drive RMS ~8.3

This is the BEST result in the entire study -- including ch7/8, which never
dropped below ~9 dB in ANY other configuration tested all session (nominal
system, two-mode switch, all-modes switch, every averaging config in Part
A). Dramatically better than both the all-modes SWITCHED result (B.2/B.3:
3.6-4.9 dB) and even the nominal system's own best result. This flips the
B.3 conclusion: the all-modes-shifted system is not inherently harder to
control than nominal -- if anything it may be easier (plausibly the random
mode shifts moved whatever antiresonance null was hurting ch7/8 -- nodes
13/14 -- in the nominal system). The ~0.5-1.5 dB switched-run cost was a
scheduler artifact, not a property of the shifted system. See B.5 for the
mechanism.

--- B.5 Mechanism: why the switched run got stuck (scheduler blind spot) ---
_refine_batch only has two ways to touch a bin's solution:
  - Tier 2 (worst-error-first): only considers bins where sdp_refined==False.
  - Tier 1 (drift-protection): only re-solves an already-refined bin if its
    MEASURED H has drifted more than frf_update_threshold (5% relative
    Frobenius norm) since it was last solved.

Fresh start: every bin begins sdp_refined=False. Tier 1 has nothing to do,
so the full max_bins_per_update budget (20/call) goes to tier 2 -- a genuine
worst-error-first sweep across the TRUE shifted system, unclouded by any
history. Every bin eventually gets a real SDP solve against the actual
current H.

Switched run: before the switch, the control law had already fully
converged against the OLD (nominal) system -- n_deferred==0, all 901 bins
marked sdp_refined=True. After the switch, tier 2 can NEVER fire again
(nothing left in not_refined) -- the only path back to re-optimization is
tier 1, capped at HALF the budget (10/call, our earlier fix) and gated on
the 5% drift threshold.

That gate is the actual bug: if a bin's true H changes enough to hurt its
tracking accuracy but by LESS than 5% in Frobenius norm, it is never
flagged as drifted, so it is NEVER re-solved -- it sits on its stale
pre-switch solution indefinitely, no matter how long the test runs, because
nothing in the scheduler ever asks "is this bin's ACHIEVED ERROR bad" for
an already-refined bin -- only "has its H moved enough". Given the all-modes
shift moved 33 modes by modest amounts (+/-20 Hz, +/-25% damping, mostly
away from any one bin's resonant peak), it's very plausible many bins moved
just enough to matter for accuracy but not enough to cross the 5% trigger.
Those bins are permanently orphaned after a switch in a way they are never
orphaned on a fresh start. Fix: see Part C.

PART C: SCHEDULER FIX (orphaned-bin blind spot from B.5) -- CONFIRMED WORKING
===============================================================================
Implemented in control_laws/optimal_diagonal_control.py: a new step 3 in
_refine_batch spends whatever budget steps 1-2 didn't use re-triaging
ALREADY-refined bins by predicted error, not H-drift magnitude. Critically,
staleness is judged against each bin's OWN error at ITS OWN last solve
(new err_db_cache, updated by every step whenever a bin is solved) --
NOT the absolute error_threshold_db. First implementation used the absolute
threshold and was caught in verification before ever running live: at
convergence on the static nominal system, 841 of 901 bins sit permanently
above error_threshold_db (they're near-null/hard-limited, not stale), so
that version would have wasted its whole budget forever re-solving bins
that can never improve, instead of the genuinely-degraded ones. Fixed to
compare against err_db_cache + error_threshold_db (bin must have gotten
WORSE by more than the threshold since its own last solve) before ever
testing live.

Verified in two stages:
  1. Offline: static H (no drift) -> stale_resolved stays 0 for the entire
     run, confirming zero behavior change for ordinary fresh-start
     convergence (matches pre-fix numbers exactly: buzz=5.749,
     optimal-diagonal=5.724 RMS dB, identical to before).
  2. Offline: 2% random H perturbation (under the 5% drift threshold, so
     tier 1 never fires) -> step 3 found and fixed 4 genuinely-degraded
     bins in the first call, then correctly stayed at n_stale_deferred=0
     for 14 more calls -- catches real degradation without getting stuck
     re-trying bins that can't improve.

Live confirmation: re-ran the all-modes SWITCH experiment (Exponential
a=0.15, TF-update ON) with the fix in place.
  - Right after switch: no jump (as before the fix).
  - ~14 calls later: still flat (as before the fix) -- looked identical to
    the pre-fix run at this point, which was misleading; the real
    difference shows up over a longer horizon.
  - Over hundreds of subsequent calls, n_stale_deferred (the backlog of
    degraded-but-unfixed bins) DOES build up for a while -- tier 1
    permanently claims half the budget for ongoing closed-loop noise
    (drifted_resolved=10/call, every call, the whole run), so tier 3 only
    ever gets the other half to clear the switch-induced backlog. Backlog
    peaked around n_stale_deferred~409 (call #663) then visibly drained
    (409 -> 307 over the next 14 calls, self_predicted error improving
    over that same window) -- NOT stuck, just budget-constrained and
    slower than the switch transient itself.
  - Settled result (~21 minutes post-switch, drive RMS ~5.9):
        ch2-6 = 2.04 - 2.71 dB, ch7-8 = 6.81 - 8.68 dB

Comparison, confirms the fix:
    Fresh start (no switch):              ch2-6 1.85-2.56, ch7-8 5.1-7.9
    Switched, PRE-fix:                    ch2-6 3.6-4.9,   ch7-8 ~9-25 (never recovered)
    Switched, POST-fix:                   ch2-6 2.04-2.71, ch7-8 6.81-8.68

Post-fix switched result lands within noise of the fresh-start result on
ch2-6, and in the same range on ch7-8 -- a dramatic improvement over the
pre-fix run, which never recovered on ch7-8 at all. Confirms the B.5
diagnosis: the orphaned-bin scheduler blind spot, not anything about the
shifted system itself, was the real cause of the original degradation.
Recovery is not instantaneous (took most of the ~21-minute run to fully
settle, constrained by tier 1's permanent half-budget claim) but it does
converge to essentially the same place a fresh start reaches.

===============================================================================
PART E: HOW MUCH DOES THE CAP ACTUALLY BITE? Achieved coherence + 0.5 cap run
===============================================================================
Question raised: does lower coherence eventually cost accuracy, as intuition
suggests? The single 0.9-vs-0.95 comparison in Part D couldn't distinguish
"no such cost exists" from "haven't tightened the cap enough to find it yet".

--- E.1 What coherence is actually being achieved? ---
Loaded a saved live run (six12system8cont.95corrcapdrive.nc4, 0.95 cap,
nominal system, "Save Current Spectral Data") and computed the REAL achieved
drive-to-drive coherence directly from the measured drive_cpsd (901 in-band
bins x 15 drive pairs):
    mean=0.58, median=0.60, 95th pct=0.94, 99th pct=0.98, max=0.9999
    fraction > 0.90: 9.7%,  fraction > 0.94: 4.7%

Most bins sit well under EITHER cap (0.9 or 0.95) -- only the top ~10% tail
was anywhere near 0.90, under 5% near 0.94. That's why 0.9 vs 0.95 barely
moved anything in Part D: neither cap was constraining the bulk of the band,
only a small tail. (Max=0.9999, above the nominal 0.95 cap, is expected and
not a bug -- the cap only applies to SDP-refined bins; any bin still on the
uncapped buzz baseline at save time can show coherence near 1.0.)

Checked whether high-coherence bins cluster at modal frequencies (a
reasonable physical hypothesis): weakly true in bulk (mean coherence within
3 Hz of a mode: 0.593 vs 0.580 away -- small), but the EXTREME tail tells a
sharper story. The top-coherence bins cluster specifically in two DENSELY
MODAL regions -- 100-250 Hz (five modes packed together: 120, 159.79,
214.37, 246.94, 249.18 Hz) and 610-656 Hz (three modes: 610.78, 620.29,
654.36 Hz) -- and typically sit 10-16 Hz OFF a mode peak, not on it.
Interpretation: it's not proximity to any single mode that demands high
coherence, it's discriminating between several CLOSELY-SPACED modes at
once (hitting a flat target near each without letting neighbors bleed into
each other) that does -- a more specific, and more demanding, mechanism
than simple resonance proximity.

--- E.2 Cap = 0.5 (nominal system, no switch) ---
Since the achieved coherence is mostly under 0.6, a cap of 0.5 should
finally bite into a meaningful fraction of the band rather than just the
tail -- the right test of whether accuracy eventually suffers.

  Cap  | ch2-6 range (dB) | Drive RMS
  0.95 |    3.05 - 3.48   |   ~3.85
  0.9  |    1.99 - 2.67   |   ~3.73
  0.5  |    1.89 - 2.86   |   ~4.69   (~9 min, not fully confirmed settled;
                                        user later flagged the Exponential
                                        coefficient may have been set wrong
                                        for this run, not confirmed =0.15 --
                                        drive-RMS figure here is suspect,
                                        treat as unconfirmed/possibly confounded)

Result: accuracy is STILL not meaningfully worse than 0.9 -- essentially the
same range. What moved is drive RMS, up ~25% from the 0.9-cap level. This
extends the pattern already seen in the offline capped-vs-uncapped
investigation (0.95 vs. fully uncapped: same accuracy, +20% drive RMS) all
the way down to 0.5: tightening the coherence constraint keeps getting paid
for in drive effort, not tracking accuracy, at least across the whole range
tested so far (0.5-1.0). The SDP appears to reliably find an alternative,
less-correlated drive combination that still hits the diagonal target, just
at higher power cost.

Qualitative observation on the live plot: overall/average error is close to
the 0.9-cap run, but a few individual control-signal traces show more
pronounced outlier spikes than at 0.9 -- consistent with the cap now
genuinely constraining a handful of specific bins (rather than zero bins,
as at 0.9) enough to show up as visible, if not RMS-dominant, excursions.

--- E.3 Cap = 0.25 (nominal system, no switch, Exponential coef confirmed 0.15) ---
Tighter still, and this time BOTH numbers moved in the direction the
original intuition expected:

  Cap  | ch2-6 range (dB) | Drive RMS
  0.95 |    3.05 - 3.48   |   ~3.85
  0.9  |    1.99 - 2.67   |   ~3.73
  0.5  |    1.89 - 2.86   |   ~4.69   (coefficient unconfirmed, see E.2 note)
  0.25 |    2.64 - 3.46   |   ~4.80   (~10 min, coefficient confirmed 0.15)

At 0.25, error crept back up past the 0.9 result to roughly the 0.95-cap
level, AND drive RMS is now the highest of the confirmed-coefficient runs.
This is the first clean sign of a genuine accuracy cost, not just a
drive-RMS cost -- consistent with the original intuition that a tight
enough cap should eventually hurt tracking, not only cost more power. The
0.5-cap result's flat accuracy may partly reflect its unconfirmed
coefficient rather than the cap alone; 0.25 is the first data point in this
sweep with a clean, confirmed setup showing real movement.

Worth a finer sweep (0.6, 0.4, 0.15, 0.1) with confirmed settings and full
settle times per point to map out where the crossover actually sits and how
sharp it is.

PART F: SDP SPEEDUP (cached DPP problem + CLARABEL) -- 2026-08-16
===============================================================================
Motivation: with the coherence-cap and scheduler work done, asked whether
the per-bin SDP solve itself (control_laws/optimal_diagonal_control.py's
_solve_one_bin) could be sped up -- each call was rebuilding a fresh
cp.Variable/cp.Problem from scratch (DCP canonicalization overhead) and
solving with SCS. Two "quick win" changes, no algorithmic/formulation
change to the optimization itself:

  1. Reuse a single parametrized cvxpy Problem across every bin solve
     instead of rebuilding one per call. H can't be a direct cp.Parameter
     -- diag(H X H^H) is bilinear in H (H appears on both sides of X),
     which isn't DPP-affine-in-parameter and would either error or silently
     defeat the caching. Reformulated instead: each response channel m's
     diagonal term is a linear functional of X,
         diag(H X H^H)[m] = h_m X h_m^H = real(sum(W_m .* X)),
         W_m = outer(h_m, conj(h_m))
     so W_m (computed in plain numpy per bin, negligible cost) is what's
     fed in as the Parameter -- a standard "coefficient .* variable"
     pattern DPP accepts. Problem is built once (verified
     prob.is_dcp(dpp=True)), reused via .value updates + warm_start=True.
  2. Solver: SCS -> CLARABEL (already installed, zero new dependencies).

Verified offline against examples/sixdrive12resp/code/
compare_buzz_vs_optimal_diagonal.py (the existing 901-bin, F=100-1000Hz,
M=8/N=6 validation harness) before touching the live code path:
  - Accuracy unchanged: RMS dB error 5.682 (new) vs 5.724 (old baseline),
    within solver-choice noise. Zero solver failures either way.
  - Speed: 56.6s -> 9.4s end-to-end for full 901-bin convergence, ~6x
    faster. An isolated per-solve microbenchmark (random 8x6 H, 8 trials)
    showed ~7.5x per-solve speedup once the one-time problem-build cost is
    amortized (mean solve time 54.0ms -> 6.9ms).
Committed as 3371ac50.

--- F.1 Live confirmation, baseline system, max_bins_per_update=20 (default) ---
Same steady-state accuracy as before the speedup: Response Error settled at
[5.679, 1.791, 2.644, 2.242, 2.065, 2.073, 11.910, 9.122] dB (matches the
pre-speedup run's [5.612-5.76, 1.78-1.9, 2.49-2.64, 2.1-2.2, 1.8-2.1,
1.85-2.1, 11.9-12.0, 9.3-9.4] range closely). What changed is wall-clock:
Total Test Time to reach this steady state was 2:03, versus ~4-6 minutes
remembered from pre-speedup runs at the same 20-bins/loop setting. Same
number of _refine_batch calls needed to cover all 901 bins either way (the
budget-per-call, not solve speed, sets that count) -- the win is purely
each call's SDP solves now taking a fraction of the wall-clock time they
used to.

--- F.2 Live test, baseline system, max_bins_per_update=100 (bumped from 20) ---
Motivation: now that each solve is ~7x cheaper, is there a benefit to
spending more SDP budget per call? Set via the GUI's Control Parameters
field (confirmed the field, not just the saved .xlsx, has to be set live --
editing the profile .xlsx's Control Parameters cell alone did NOT take
effect on an already-running/already-loaded environment; had to type it
directly into the GUI field and confirmed via a new
"[optimal_diagonal_control] __init__:" log line showing
max_bins_per_update=100 before trusting the run).

Result: bin COVERAGE reached full (901/901) dramatically faster --
by call #17, versus call #46 at the 20-bins/loop setting. But steady-state
ACCURACY was worse, not better: settled self-predicted RMS
[5.91, 3.24, 3.73, 3.68, 3.57, 3.05, 11.74, 9.36] dB vs the 20-bins/loop
plateau of [5.75-5.76, 1.78-1.84, 2.49-2.64, 2.12-2.17, 1.83-2.04,
1.85-2.06, 11.92-11.95, 9.27-9.34] dB -- roughly 1.5-2x worse on the
well-coupled channels (2-6).

Working hypothesis: "Update Sys ID During Control" was ON for this test
(user's choice, to keep it comparable to the drift-driven live conditions
of the earlier FRF-change work), and the live H1 estimate under that
setting carries the closed-loop identification bias documented in
frf_change_study_2026-08-14.txt section 3 (H1 drifts away from truth
because the excitation is shaped by the control law, not broadband -- not
a real system change). The per-call drift-protected re-solve budget is
capped at half of max_bins_per_update, so raising the cap 20->100 means
already-refined bins get re-solved against that biased H far more often
per unit time (cum_frf_updates hit 5850 by call #119 at max=100, vs only
2150 by call #217 at max=20 -- i.e. roughly 25x more re-solves per unit
wall-clock time). More aggressive re-solving against a systematically
biased (not just noisy) H estimate means the scheduler chases the bias
harder, so spending more budget per call made steady-state accuracy worse
under this specific condition. This is a genuine, reproducible tradeoff
introduced by the speedup making re-solves cheap enough to spend
liberally, not a bug in the speedup itself.

NOT yet isolated from the "is 100 just worse in general" question -- a
TF-update-OFF (static H) run at max_bins_per_update=100 would isolate
whether more budget/loop is actually neutral-to-helpful absent the closed-
loop bias, or whether something else about 100 is worse independent of
that. Flagged as open, not run (user chose to keep TF-update on for this
round, and then asked to revert to the confirmed-good 20-bins/loop
setting instead).

Recommendation: keep max_bins_per_update at the default (20) when
TF-update is on. The speedup's main practical benefit under that condition
is wall-clock (4-6min -> ~2min to converge), not "raise the budget" --
raising the budget interacts badly with the known closed-loop bias.

PART G: GENUINELY NONLINEAR (AMPLITUDE-DEPENDENT) FRF -- 2026-08-20/21
===============================================================================
Motivation: Parts B/C's FRF-change scenarios switch between two FIXED
systems at a trigger -- useful, but not the same thing as a real structural
nonlinearity where the FRF continuously depends on how hard the system is
currently being driven. Built a genuinely nonlinear variant instead: same
6-drive/12-response geometry, same linear M/K/C, plus an amplitude-
dependent softening stiffness + damping increase on mode 1 (120 Hz, 4%
baseline damping) in modal coordinates:
    q'' + 2*zeta0*wn*q' + wn^2*q + k3*q^3 + c2*q'*|q'| = Qn(t)
projected onto physical DOFs via the mode's own mass-normalized
eigenvector (same rank-1 M-orthogonal projection idea as Part A's shifted
systems, just nonlinear/state-dependent instead of a constant shift).
k3 (softening, <0) gives frequency drop scaling ~amplitude^2; c2 gives
damping increase scaling ~amplitude. Calibrated numerically (free-decay
root-solving on the isolated SDOF, not first-order Duffing formulas) to
target roughly -4% frequency / +20% damping change between a reference
modal amplitude and 2x that amplitude -- landed close but not exact by
design (frequency and damping scale differently with amplitude, so they
don't move together proportionally the way a single linear knob would).

New files only -- the existing linear sdynpy_system_virtual_hardware.py is
completely untouched: build_nonlinear_frf_system.py (system builder +
calibration) and components/sdynpy_nonlinear_system_virtual_hardware.py
(hardware subclass overriding only read(), since the nonlinear force is
state-dependent and scipy's lsim -- exact for LTI only -- can't handle it).

--- G.1 A real accuracy bug caught before shipping ---
A single-substep RK4 (one step per sample, ~0.195ms at 5120 Hz) disagreed
with the linear system's exact lsim baseline by ~13% (relative, on
acceleration outputs specifically -- the C matrix's omega^2-scale
coefficients amplify small state errors from under-resolving the ~900 Hz
top mode). Fixed with 4 RK4 substeps between each reported sample: matches
lsim to ~0.05% relative, consistent with RK4's expected 4th-order
convergence. Real-time budget checked: ~0.57s raw integration time within
the 1.0s frame budget at this system's sample rate -- workable, not
generous, since substeps make this meaningfully more expensive than the
linear system's near-instant lsim.

--- G.2 Reference-amplitude calibration was initially unrealistic ---
First calibration picked a reference modal amplitude (Q0_REF=1.0, abstract
mass-normalized units) with no connection to what the system could
actually reach -- turned out to need ~81,000 V RMS-equivalent drive to hit,
useless for a live demo. Also found a uniform in-phase drive across all 6
shakers barely couples into mode 1 at all (its modal participation factor
for that mode shape is tiny) -- had to shape the open-loop characterization
drive proportional to the mode's own shape at the drive points (standard
single-mode-excitation practice) to get any real coupling. Recalibrated
Q0_REF against an actual resonant-response formula at a chosen reference
drive level (20 V RMS, mode-shaped), landing on a sensible working range:
negligible nonlinearity below ~4V, mild 8-20V, clear 24-28V (open-loop
sine-drive characterization -- the live closed-loop number differs some,
see G.4).

--- G.3 Hardware dispatch wiring -- three separate gaps, all fixed ---
Registering the new hardware type (index 7) required touching four spots,
not the one expected:
  1. components/acquisition.py / output.py -- dispatch elif branches
     (purely additive, existing types 0-6 untouched).
  2. components/combined_environments_controller.ui -- the Hardware Type
     combobox has no dynamic lookup; its item ORDER directly maps to the
     hardware index, so an unlisted index 7 had nothing to select and
     silently fell back toward index 6 with no error.
  3. components/user_interface.py's hardware_update() -- a hardcoded
     if/elif chain per index (for showing/hiding the right widgets and
     prompting for a file) that falls through to an "Invalid Hardware
     Type! ... How did you do this?!" dialog for any unhandled index.
  4. The same file's `in [4,5,6]` grouping (reads sample_rate/
     output_oversample from the state-space-style widgets) -- missing 7
     there would have silently used output_oversample=1 regardless of the
     configured value, a correctness bug rather than a hard error.
Also hit the standard "the loaded profile doesn't refresh an already-open
GUI session" issue from earlier parts of this study -- had to reload the
profile and re-select the hardware type explicitly in a couple of cases
even after the file itself was correct.

--- G.4 Live confirmation: control tracks the nonlinearity across an 8x
    drive-level swing ---
Ran optimal_diagonal_control (unmodified, coherence-capped SDP) against
the nonlinear system, TF-update ON, at Target Test Level -6dB, 0dB, then
+12dB (roughly an 8x swing in actual drive amplitude). Response Error (dB)
stayed essentially IDENTICAL across all three levels:
    -6dB: [5.37, 1.52, 2.46, 2.11, 1.93, 1.93, 11.69, 9.10]
     0dB: [5.31, 1.56, 2.38, 2.04, 1.93, 1.85, 11.64, 9.06]
   +12dB: [5.40, 1.55, 2.46, 2.07, 1.94, 1.92, 11.72, 9.09]
Live log confirmed continuous H_changed_since_last=True with steady
drifted_resolved=10/call throughout -- i.e. this flat error is NOT because
nothing was happening; the live FRF genuinely was drifting under the
amplitude-dependent nonlinearity, and the scheduler's drift-protected
re-solve was actively, successfully compensating for it the whole time.
This is the clean, positive demonstration this whole build was for.

Along the way, found (and want on record so it isn't re-discovered as a
surprise later): the "Output Voltages (RMS)" GUI panel is computed
(random_vibration_sys_id_data_analysis.py) directly from the control
law's raw, PRE-test-level-scale output_cpsd -- it does not reflect actual
test level at all, by design elsewhere in the existing (untouched)
codebase, not a bug introduced here. Confirmed the ACTUAL drive signal
really is scaled correctly via Rattlesnake.log's dedicated per-process log
("Random Signal Generation -- Test Level at 3.981...", matching
db2scale(12dB) exactly) -- gui_debug.log (raw stdout capture) does not
carry these messages; Rattlesnake.log (a separate file, written by a
dedicated logging process) does.

--- G.5 Post-run validation from saved spectral data ---
Saved a .nc4 snapshot at +12dB and checked it offline. First pass raised a
false alarm: comparing the achieved drive CPSD's modal-force projection
directly against the stability threshold suggested the system was ~11,000x
past the point where the softening spring's restoring force flips sign
(runaway). That comparison was wrong -- it skipped the resonant
amplification factor (q_ss = Q0/(2*zeta*wn^2)) that converts modal FORCE
into modal RESPONSE amplitude, the two are very different scales.
Corrected: achieved modal amplitude q_ss ~ 1.02x the calibration reference
(right at the intended operating point) and only ~0.25x the stability
limit -- comfortably stable, not a runaway. Lesson: check units before
raising an alarm from a quick calculation, even under time pressure.

Also checked whether the softened mode 1 frequency is actually visible in
the measured FRF (not just inferred from the fact that error stayed
controlled). A broad 90-180 Hz peak search initially found nothing near
120 Hz -- mode 2's larger, sharper peak elsewhere was dominating that
naive top-2-peaks-in-a-window search. Narrowing to 95-135 Hz specifically
found it: response channels 7 and 8 (the project's known persistent
structural weak spot, already flagged in earlier parts of this study) show
the clearest signature, peaking at 117 Hz against drive 1 -- a ~2.5%
downward shift from the 120 Hz linear baseline, consistent with the
calibrated softening effect at this achieved amplitude. Channels 1 and 2
against drive 1 show a smaller ~1% shift (121 Hz). The nonlinearity is
real, working, comfortably stable, and now confirmed in live measured
data, not just inferred.

PART H: ALL-33-MODES NONLINEARITY -- 2026-08-21
===============================================================================
Motivation: Part G's single-mode nonlinearity is a clean, easy-to-verify
demo, but a real test article's nonlinearity won't confine itself to one
known mode. Built a generalized version to check whether optimal_
diagonal_control's live drift-tracking (validated in G.4 against one
shifting mode) still holds up when it doesn't know -- and isn't told --
how many modes are moving or by how much.

--- H.1 Build ---
Generalized both the hardware class and the system builder to an
arbitrary number of target modes (vectorized throughout -- a single mode
is just the n_modes=1 case, verified byte-for-byte backward compatible
with Part G's existing single-mode file). New build script perturbs all
33 flexible modes: first 4 deterministic (matching Part G's mode-1
target), remaining 29 randomized (fixed seed), each independently
calibrated via the same free-decay root-solving as Part G, generalized to
be robust across a real spread of (frequency, damping, target)
combinations rather than hand-tuned for one mode's numbers.

29/33 modes calibrated successfully; 4 modes (7, 22, 30, 33) hit a
numerical edge case in the root-solving brackets for their randomized
targets and fell back to k3=c2=0 (linear, no nonlinearity) for that mode
specifically, with a clear warning rather than crashing the whole build --
deliberate graceful degradation for a demo/exploration tool, not silently
hidden. Real-time budget with all 33 modes active: ~0.84s of the 1.0s
frame budget (tighter than the single-mode case's ~0.57s, as expected
with 33x the modal projection work each substep, but still within
budget).

--- H.2 Live confirmation: same result holds with all modes in play ---
Ran optimal_diagonal_control against the all-modes system, TF-update ON,
at -6dB then +12dB (same protocol as G.4). Response Error (dB) again
stayed essentially flat across the level swing:
    -6dB:  [5.41, 1.61, 2.61, 2.20, 1.92, 1.76, 11.69, 9.07]
   +12dB:  [5.52, 1.60, 2.58, 2.17, 1.80, 1.69, 11.82, 9.17]
Confirmed genuinely active tracking via the log, not a null result:
H_changed_since_last=True continuously through the whole +12dB dwell,
cum_frf_updates=16,480, and Step 3's stale-error re-triage also
periodically engaging (stale_resolved=1-2 on several calls) -- the full
scheduler working, not idling.

This is the key result: the scheduler doesn't need to know how many modes
are shifting or which ones. The same generic "re-solve whatever the live
H says has drifted" mechanism that worked for one known mode in G.4 holds
up unchanged when all 33 modes are independently, unpredictably in play --
directly answering the concern that a single-mode demo might not
generalize to a realistic laboratory nonlinearity.

--- H.3 Post-hoc modal shift check -- real signal, but a real analysis
    limitation worth recording ---
Saved .nc4 snapshots at -6dB (fullnonlinearminus6dbspectra.nc4) and +12dB
(plus12dbmultinonlinearsystem.nc4) and searched the measured FRF near each
mode's baseline frequency for a peak shift between the two levels
(auto-selecting, per mode, whichever response/drive channel pair showed
the strongest peak at -6dB). 24/33 modes checked showed a >0.5 Hz shift.

That aggregate number is trustworthy; the PER-MODE breakdown mostly is
not. With 33 modes spread over 100-900 Hz, many sit close enough together
that the search windows overlap, and the "peak" found is often the same
shared nearby feature claimed by several different target frequencies --
visible directly in the results as literal duplicate (peak_lo, peak_hi)
pairs across neighboring modes (e.g. modes 6/7/8 all reporting the exact
same 301->305 Hz peak; modes 25-30, six different target modes, all
reporting the same 778->783 Hz peak). Cross-checking against modes with
NO nonlinearity assigned (the 4 failed-calibration modes) makes the
problem concrete: modes 22 and 33 both show large apparent shifts
(+15 Hz, +6 Hz) despite having k3=c2=0 -- pure neighbor bleed-through,
not a real effect, and a useful negative control for how much to trust
this method generally.

Of the 24 flagged rows, only about 7 look like independently-resolved
detections rather than duplicates -- modes 2, 3, 9, 16, 31, 32 (and
arguably 19, which conflicts with its own neighbors 20/21 despite
overlapping search windows, so is ambiguous rather than clean):

    mode 2  (159.8 Hz):  +1.0 Hz
    mode 3  (214.4 Hz):  -2.0 Hz
    mode 9  (335.2 Hz):  -6.0 Hz
    mode 16 (478.7 Hz):  +1.0 Hz
    mode 31 (837.4 Hz):  +8.0 Hz
    mode 32 (860.6 Hz): +17.0 Hz

These magnitudes (roughly 1-17 Hz, i.e. a fraction of a percent up to a
few percent depending on the mode) are in the right ballpark for what was
calibrated and read as real. Mode 1 itself, oddly, does NOT look clean in
this particular pass (found a peak at 130 Hz at -6dB, well off both the
120 Hz linear baseline and the clean 117-121 Hz found in G.5's dedicated
single-mode-only analysis) -- likely contamination from mode 2 at the
window edge, a good illustration of exactly the failure mode described
above rather than a real discrepancy in the underlying physics.

Takeaway: a simple per-mode peak search is the wrong tool once modes are
closely spaced (true for most of this system above ~400 Hz) -- it needs
either isolated modal-coordinate projection (the technique G.5 used
successfully for the single clean mode-1 case, sidesteps the window-
overlap problem entirely by using the known mode shape instead of a
generic frequency window) generalized across all 33 modes, or a proper
multi-mode curve fit, neither of which has been built yet. The live
Response Error result (H.2) remains the reliable, already-trustworthy
measurement of what actually matters here; this offline check is useful
corroboration that SOMETHING shifted broadly across the band, not a
precise characterization of what shifted where.
